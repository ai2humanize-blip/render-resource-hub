A closer look at How to Tell Explainer Journalism from Opinion

The web rewards readers who slow down, ask questions, and know how to weigh what they discover.

Explainer journalism is a form of reporting that seeks to provide a comprehensive understanding of complex issues, events, or topics. Its primary goal is to inform readers by breaking down intricate subjects into digestible, clear, and contextualized information. This type of journalism frequently involves the following characteristics:

  • Objective Analysis: Explainer journalism aims to present facts and analysis without bias. The focus is on providing a balanced view of the subject matter.
  • Contextual Background: It provides historical context, background information, and relevant data to help readers grasp the broader implications of the topic.
  • Clarity and Accessibility: The language used is straightforward and accessible, ensuring that complex topics are understandable to a wide audience.
  • Expert Input: Often includes insights from experts or individuals with deep knowledge of the subject to add depth and credibility.

for instance, if a news outlet publishes an explainer on climate change, it would likely include scientific data, historical trends, and expert opinions to provide a comprehensive overview of the issue.

In the ever-evolving landscape of media and news consumption, distinguishing between different types of journalistic content is crucial for informed decision-making. Two common forms of journalism that often get confused are explainer journalism and opinion pieces. While both aim to inform, they serve distinct purposes and employ different methods. Understanding the differences between them can significantly enhance your ability to critically assess the information you consume.

An example of opinion journalism would be a columnist's article arguing for stricter environmental regulations, where the author presents their case based on personal analysis and interpretation of data.

  • Subjective Perspective: The content is inherently biased, as it reflects the author's personal beliefs, interpretations, and judgments.
  • Argumentative Nature: Opinion pieces often involve arguments, critiques, or advocacy for a particular position or course of action.
  • Engagement and Discussion: The aim is to engage readers in a dialogue, encouraging them to think critically about the issue at hand and possibly form their own opinions.
  • Editorial Judgment: The inclusion of the author's editorial judgment is a hallmark, as they weigh evidence and present a case for their viewpoint.
